Standards and Compliance Overview

Purpose​

This page is the landing point for KE's posture toward open scholarly standards — what the platform commits to, what ships today, and what is honestly still on the way. Read it if you are a librarian or data curator asking whether exports will outlive the platform, an institutional reviewer or grants office looking for the interoperability story, an environment administrator deciding what your knowledge environment inherits without configuration, or a research team weighing the platform for a future project. Each section deepens progressively; the table is the heart of the page.

Standards adoption matters here because three things are true at once. Interoperability with the FAIR data ecosystem — findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable — is non-negotiable for an academic platform. Research data will outlast the platform that produced it, so the formats it lives in must be readable by tools the platform does not control. And scholarly citation depends on identifiers, vocabularies, and packaging that the wider research community already trusts — an identifier only the platform understands is not a citation, it is a private bookmark.

Standards are the shared foundation; a research team's interpretation rides on top of it. Voice, vocabulary, and scholarly choices stay specific to each knowledge environment — the standards layer is what makes those choices legible outside the environment's own walls.

Features​

The platform distinguishes four maturity tiers, so that "we comply with X" never has to be read on faith:

  • Built — shipped in production code and exercised by at least one feature you can see today. Removing it would break something visible.
  • Active — partially implemented and in real use. You can configure against the standard, but the full export or rendering pipeline is not yet complete end to end.
  • Validated — the platform has researched the standard, committed to its shape in a recorded design decision, and can defend the choice in front of a partner — but no implementation ships yet. Validated means committed, not built.
  • Deferred — recognised as relevant; no commitment and no implementation until a research project or a partner contract makes the need concrete. Listed openly so the absence is visible rather than silent.

The selection of standards in the table is itself deliberate. KE's scope spans text, structured data, geographic and temporal modelling, persistent identity, accessibility, and data protection — each with its own established body of standards. The table covers the standards the platform has committed to, surveyed in depth, or recognised as a near-future need. Standards outside this scope (medical imaging's DICOM, financial reporting's XBRL, music encoding's MEI) are not listed; if a future research project requires one, the same sequence applies before it enters this table — research first, then a recorded decision, then implementation.

Usability — how you use it​

How compliance is enforced​

Compliance here is structural, not declarative. Any platform can claim conformance on a slide deck; what matters is whether drift from a declared standard fails visibly or hides as documentation rot. KE's posture is that drift should fail loudly — when data is saved, when a record is published, or when it is exported.

Five mechanisms carry that posture:

  • Standards declarations on fields. Fields in an environment's data model can declare the standards they conform to — a TEI element, a CIDOC-CRM class, an IIIF presentation shape. The declaration travels with the data through publication and export, so an outside reader can see what the data claims about itself without guessing.
  • A vetted standards list. Environments declare conformance only against standards the platform has actually surveyed. Adding a new standard follows a fixed sequence: research first, then a recorded decision, then implementation.
  • A coverage matrix. Every surveyed standard is mapped against every family of concepts the platform models, cell by cell, so each conformance statement traces back to recorded evidence rather than to a slogan.
  • Validation at publication time. Before a published record's export goes out, automated checks refuse any claim of full coverage of a standard that the platform cannot actually validate. These checks run today for citation, image-annotation, and linked-data shapes; machine-runnable validation suites for TEI and CIDOC are planned, and they gate any public conformance claim for those two standards.
  • Gates in the development process. Automated checks keep environment-specific vocabulary out of shared platform code, so the conformance machinery every environment relies on stays identical for all of them.

Who reads what​

If you are a researcher. Your knowledge environment inherits scholarly citation and standards-grade export commitments without any configuration on your part. Your interpretive decisions are preserved when your source data is re-imported, citations render in your environment's chosen style, and the export formats in the table above are how your data is designed to travel to your institutional repository. See Citation Integrity and DOI Safety for the citation guarantee and Voice and Vocabulary for how your wording stays yours.

If you are an environment administrator. Standards and Citations covers what your environment gets without configuration, what you can configure — citation style, vocabulary grounding against external authorities, standards declarations — and how your data exports. Vocabulary Grounding carries the operational steps.

If you are a librarian, data curator, or institutional reviewer. The table above and the Pending and deferred section together are the honest inventory: what ships, what is committed, and what is recognised but not started. The versioning rules below explain how an export made today stays legible in ten years.

If you are a research team evaluating the platform. The maturity tags tell you what you can rely on today versus what is planned. If a standard your project requires is at Validated or Deferred, ask the platform operator for an explicit timeline; the openly recorded gap is the basis for that conversation.

What a new knowledge environment inherits​

When a research team's environment is set up, the adoption decisions in the table are already made. The environment's fields can declare conformance against the accepted standards list; its published records flow through the same publication pipeline as every other environment's; its vocabulary can ground against the same external authorities — Wikidata and PeriodO — as everyone else's.

What the environment configures is layered on top: citation style, vocabulary grounding choices, standards declarations, and its own voice and wording. The foundation is shared; the interpretation belongs to the research team.

The practical sequence for a new environment:

  1. The environment's concept families are configured from the platform's shared field types. Fields can optionally declare which accepted standards they conform to.
  2. Vocabulary grounding — Wikidata for entities, PeriodO for periods — is offered as a guided workflow; the environment administrator reviews proposed matches and confirms the ones that fit.
  3. A citation style is selected; the choice flows into every surface where the platform renders citations.
  4. Published records flow through the shared publication pipeline. As each export format — JSON-LD, RO-Crate, TEI — reaches Built maturity, it becomes available to every environment at once, with no environment-specific code.

When standards compose or disagree​

No single standard covers everything a research collection needs to express. A place record may carry CIDOC-CRM for cultural-heritage modelling, GeoSPARQL for geometry, schema.org markup for search-engine visibility, Pleiades or Wikidata identifiers for external identity, and a JSON-LD context for serialisation — all at once. The platform's shared definitions are designed around composition: a single definition can declare conformance with several standards at once, and the export pipeline is designed to carry each conformance statement alongside the others.

When two standards model the same thing with different shapes — a historical period as a CIDOC-CRM period, a Dublin Core period of time, or a SKOS concept — the platform is designed to express each shape from the same underlying record rather than picking one and discarding the others. A researcher does not have to choose which standard their data complies with.

When a standard is silent on something the platform needs to express, the gap is recorded explicitly, and the extension lives as a clearly separate field rather than as a falsified conformance claim.

Versioning the standards we adopt​

Standards themselves evolve. TEI is on P5; IIIF Presentation is on 3.0; CIDOC-CRM is on 7.1.3; WCAG is on 2.2. KE pins each adoption to a specific version of the standard, recorded with the adoption decision, and exported data is designed to carry that version pin in its conformance statements — so a future reader can resolve the exact specification the data was produced against. This is what keeps a 2026 export legible in 2036: the version pin is the contract.

Pending and deferred​

Items where adoption is recognised as relevant but not yet committed, or committed but not yet finished — listed openly so the absence is visible:

  • Darwin Core — no current research project requires biodiversity-grade modelling of taxa and specimens. It becomes a candidate when a project with specimen-level or species-level data joins the platform and the evidence review supports adoption.
  • ICU (Unicode collation and folding) — sorting and accent handling currently use per-field folding rules over the database's and the search engine's built-in facilities. ICU becomes a candidate when a project needs Unicode-grade case or accent folding beyond those facilities, or when a partner requires ICU conformance in a contract.
  • OpenAPI — GraphQL is the platform's primary interface. OpenAPI becomes a candidate if a partner institution requires it for automated harvesting of a specific endpoint.
  • Full WCAG 2.2 AA audit — the accessibility controls ship today; the recorded end-to-end audit is queued until the public reading surface is stable, so that the audit measures a fixed target rather than a moving one. Until that audit is recorded, the platform does not present itself as WCAG-certified.
  • CIDOC / Linked Art validation suites — machine-runnable validation tests are a precondition the platform set for itself: no public claim of CIDOC conformance until they pass. The suites are not yet written; holding the claim until they pass is deliberate.
  • TEI conformance bundle — a runnable, version-pinned validation bundle is planned once published exports are stable enough to validate against TEI. When complete, it will be published alongside this overview.

These gaps are recorded openly so that anyone evaluating the platform can read what it does not yet do alongside what it does. A known conformance gap beats an unverified conformance claim.
