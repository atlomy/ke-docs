Planning deposits and answering compliance reviews

Purpose​

Two readers arrive at this page with different questions but a shared need for an honest answer.

An institutional reviewer — a grants office, a research data manager, a data protection officer — is filling in a checklist and needs to know what the platform records, how long it keeps it, which data-protection rights it honours, where the data lives, and whether its accessibility and export claims hold up. A researcher or librarian is planning a deposit and needs to know which standards they can rely on today versus which are committed but not yet shipped, so that a deposit plan does not promise a repository something the platform cannot yet deliver.

Both questions have the same answer shape: the platform states what ships, what is committed, and what is recognised but not started — and never lets a commitment read as a delivery. This page explains how to read that posture for a compliance review or a deposit plan. It is a guide to the answers, not a separate set of guarantees; the Standards and Compliance Overview carries the authoritative maturity table, and everything here points back to it.

The single rule that governs every answer below: a maturity tag describes what ships, not what is promised. When a reviewer asks "do you comply with X?", the honest answer is the tag, plus the explicit gap where the tag is below Built.

For institutional reviewers: the checklist in the order it is asked​

A standard onboarding review tends to walk the same sequence. Here is where each answer comes from, and what the honest answer is today.

What is recorded​

The platform records a deliberately small set of operational signals about how it runs — never a copy of research content. For each AI call, each import run, and each suggestion-review decision, it keeps a structured record: the task, counts, timings, outcome flags, and one-way fingerprints of any text involved, never the raw text. Text matching a high-risk personal-data pattern — an email address, a phone number, an identification number — is dropped entirely, with no record and no fingerprint kept. The full inventory of signal classes, with what each contains and excludes, is in Telemetry and Privacy; the researcher-facing view of the same posture is in Privacy and Safety.

The default posture is capture-off: AI-call, import-run, and review-decision capture record nothing until switched on for an environment, and switching on review-decision capture additionally requires a recorded privacy acknowledgment first. The one capture class that starts on is tip-activity, which carries no researcher identity at all.

Retention​

Retention is set per data class, with defaults calibrated against operational-log guidance from data-protection regulators and the relevant information-security control set. AI-call records are kept in full for a short window, then only as a hashed aggregate; import-run records and security-relevant audit records have their own longer windows, with quarantined abuse material held shortest. The current default table — and the basis for each window — is in Telemetry and Privacy. Shorter, environment-specific retention is arranged through the platform operator.

GDPR rights​

GDPR support is Active: real and in use, with the mechanics documented per right. Access and portability are served by an operator-produced export scoped to a single environment, with a manifest. Erasure pseudonymizes user-identifying telemetry rows in place and keeps only small-count-suppressed aggregates as anonymous data. Restriction and objection are handled by per-person suppression, or by turning a signal class off at the environment level. The platform makes no automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects on a person: AI output is advisory, and researcher review gates any change to environment data. The lawful basis is legitimate interest, with a documented assessment per signal class and a data-protection impact assessment that precedes production capture and is refreshed annually. The right-by-right table and the lawful-basis detail are in Telemetry and Privacy.

Data residency and isolation​

Each environment's data lives in its own separate compartment of the database — its own schema — and the database itself enforces that isolation. Telemetry honours the same boundary: telemetry tables refuse to be read from outside an environment's schema. The platform operator holds read-only access to an environment's schema for support, and platform-level operational metrics (cost, latency, model name) that identify an environment only by an internal identifier and carry no content. No third-party analytics, advertising, or external telemetry service sees anything. Residency questions beyond this — where the underlying infrastructure runs for a given deployment — go to the platform operator, because they depend on the deployment, not on the platform code.

Accessibility status​

The honest answer is Active, not certified. Reader-facing accessibility controls ship today: a control panel for font size, line height, font family, and background-colour presets on the reading and browsing pages, plus keyboard navigation, visible keyboard-only focus rings, and screen-reader-announced hints on fields that are not yet editable. What has not happened is a recorded, end-to-end WCAG 2.2 AA audit — and until it has, the platform does not present itself as WCAG-certified. The audit is deliberately held until the public reading surface is stable, so that it measures a fixed target. The full picture of what works today, and what is still expanding (screen-reader announcement coverage, for example), is in Accessibility. For a compliance review, the correct line to record is: accessibility controls present and in use; formal AA audit not yet performed.

Export formats​

This is where deposit planning and compliance review meet, and where the maturity distinction matters most. Reading the export answer correctly is the next section.

Persistent identifiers and preservation​

Published records are designed to carry a stable, citable identity against which an external agency can register a persistent identifier. The platform does not mint those identifiers itself, and adoption of the researcher-identity and bibliographic-registration authorities is committed, not yet shipped. Research-object packaging that bundles a record with its provenance for long-term preservation is likewise committed but not yet built. The maturity tags in the Standards and Compliance Overview state exactly where each of these stands; treat the tag as the answer, and ask the platform operator for a timeline where a tag is below Built.

For researchers and librarians: planning a deposit​

A deposit plan should distinguish what you can rely on now from what you can list as committed-pending. The line runs through the maturity tag.

What you can rely on today (Built). Citation rendering through a standard processor, in your environment's chosen citation style, across publication, citation checking, search, and bibliographic export. The platform's primary read-and-write interface is a production application contract. Annotation targets on published records follow a shared web-annotation model and are shape-checked at publication time. These behave today; a deposit plan can lean on them.

What is committed but not yet shipped (Validated). The linked-data export of published records, the research-object packaging that wraps a record with its provenance, and the full text-encoding export of published records are all committed in recorded design decisions but not yet built. The same is true of the persistent-identifier and researcher-identity authorities. Validated means committed, not built — list these in a deposit plan as committed-pending, not as available formats, and confirm a timeline with the platform operator before a repository or funder depends on a date.

What is recognised but not started (Deferred). A standard listed as Deferred is recognised as potentially relevant but carries no commitment until a concrete project need or a partner contract makes it real. If a deposit depends on a Deferred standard, that is the opening for an explicit conversation with the platform operator, not an assumption to plan around.

What "Validated" means before an export reaches Built​

A reasonable deposit plan can name a Validated export format as the intended destination — but it must not assume the format produces a file today. Validated means the platform has researched the standard, committed to a specific version of it in a recorded decision, and can defend that choice — but the export pipeline that emits a conforming file is not yet complete end to end. For a deposit plan, the practical reading is: design the deposit toward the committed format, keep the plan's promised-formats list to what is Built, and treat each Validated format as a near-future addition whose date comes from the platform operator.

A second guard sits behind this. Before any published record's export goes out, automated checks refuse to claim full coverage of a standard the platform cannot actually validate. Those checks run today for citation, image-annotation, and linked-data shapes; machine-runnable validation suites for the heavier text-encoding and cultural-heritage standards are planned, and they gate any public conformance claim for those two. So an export will not silently overclaim conformance it cannot back — which is exactly the property a deposit plan needs.

When a format matures: what changes for an in-flight plan​

When a committed export format reaches Built, it becomes available to every environment at once, with no per-environment work — published records already flow through the shared publication pipeline, and the new format joins that pipeline for everyone. For an in-flight deposit plan, this means the moment of change is the format reaching Built, not a per-environment migration. The sequence to expect:

  1. The format is committed (Validated) — list it as committed-pending, design toward it.
  2. The validation suite that gates its public conformance claim is completed — at that point a full conformance claim becomes possible rather than refused.
  3. The export reaches Built — it produces a conforming file, and the deposit plan can move the format from committed-pending to available.

Each adoption is pinned to a specific version of the standard, recorded with the decision and carried in the exported data's conformance statements. A future reader can resolve the exact specification the data was produced against — which is what keeps a deposit legible long after the platform that produced it. When a standard publishes a new major version, the platform evaluates it through the same research-then-decision sequence and keeps both versions pinned during any transition, so an in-flight deposit pinned to the earlier version stays valid.

Who to ask, and how the tags map to answers​

Most of these answers are read straight off the maturity table; a few require the platform operator because they depend on the deployment or on documents held outside the published docs.

The division of labour to expect: reading the posture — which tag applies, what a signal class records, what a right entitles a person to — is self-serve from these pages. Producing artefacts that touch stored data — a GDPR export, a per-person suppression, a change to an environment's capture posture, a residency confirmation — is operator-mediated, because those actions run against the live environment and its platform record. An environment administrator initiates and routes such requests; the platform operator performs them.
