Dating Temporal Values

Purpose​

Dates in real sources are rarely tidy. A column of dates might hold 384 BCE – 322 BCE, c. 80–150, mid-4th c. BCE, a century, a dynasty, or a named period. This page explains how the workspace turns those into operable dates — years you can sort, filter, and place on a timeline — without ever rewriting what you wrote, and without converting one calendar into another. Read it when a date column has messy values, when you want your dates to line up on a timeline, or when you are deciding whether to date a value by hand.

The idea: keep the words, add the years​

Two things live side by side on a dated value, and the workspace never lets one overwrite the other:

  • What the source says — the date exactly as written. This is always kept, character for character. It is the record of what you found.
  • Years the workspace can work with — an optional, separate reading that turns the words into start and end years the platform can order and place on a timeline.

Adding the second never changes the first. You are annotating the value with dates, not editing its text. And nothing here converts calendars: if a value is written in one calendar, its years are placed on the timeline you choose, as you entered them — the platform does not silently restate a date in a different calendar.

The editors you'll see​

Which editor a date cell opens depends on the column and the value, but they all follow the same rule — keep the words, add the years — and differ only in how much structure they offer.

Date this entry — the manual form​

Most date cells open Date this entry as a manual form: you keep your value as written and enter its years — and, for a named period, a label — directly, rather than being offered readings to accept.

Date this entry — the guided version​

When a value has been flagged for grounding through the vocabulary review, the same Date this entry editor opens a guided version that shows your value at the top, kept exactly as written, and offers three ways to turn it into dates:

  • What we read — a deterministic parser's reading of your text: the years it resolves to, and the precision (for example circa). Same input, same result, no AI involved. If the text cannot be read into years — a named period, a non-Gregorian calendar — nothing is guessed; it stays as written and you use one of the other paths.
  • AI suggestion — an advisory reading, offered only when you open it. It never writes anything on its own: it proposes a label and dates for you to Accept, Edit first, or Reject.
  • Enter it yourself — type a start year (and optional end year) with BCE/CE, a precision, and a calendar. Your original text stays above the fields, read-only.

Each tab records the dates its own way: Save this match on What we read, Accept (or Edit first) on AI suggestion, and Save these dates on Enter it yourself. Leave as written is always there to close without adding any.

Edit time range​

The Edit time range editor keeps the same two-part shape explicit. As written is the reading exactly as your source gives it, and is required. Below it, an optional Make it comparable section lets you add:

  • a From year and optional To year (a plain number; negative for BCE, and a blank To year dates the start year on its own — that one year, decade, or century at the chosen precision, not a span),
  • a Precision — instant, day, month, year, decade, or century,
  • and a Comparison axis — which timeline the years sit on. It is required once you enter years; — not comparable — is only for keeping the value as written, with no years to place.

Give it no years and the value is saved as written only; a value that already has years keeps them if you save without changing them. Fill in a year, a precision, and a comparison axis together and it also becomes comparable. Enter a year but leave the axis on — not comparable — and the editor keeps Save blocked until you either complete the three or clear the years to save as written. Here too, the calendar is a label on the value while the comparison axis is what lets it be ordered against others — choosing one never converts the other.

Making a value comparable​

"Comparable" means the value carries real years placed on a stated timeline, so it can be sorted, filtered, and shown on a timeline beside other values. A value that is only kept as written is still a first-class record — it simply is not placed on a timeline until someone gives it years.

The comparison axis is the deliberate part of this: it names which timeline the years belong to, rather than assuming one. That is what keeps dates from different calendars from being quietly mixed together as though they shared a single line.

Grounding a value reaches its occurrences in one action​

There are two ways your date work is saved, and they differ in reach:

  • Editing a cell — typing years into a single time-range cell, or dating a single entry — changes that one value.
  • Grounding a value — accepting a reading for a value through the vocabulary review, where the same value occurs across many records — applies that reading across its occurrences in a single action, so you never redo the same reading record by record. Where an occurrence can't take the reading — a legacy record a structured date can't be fitted onto, for instance — it is reported back to you rather than dated; the occurrences that can take it are still dated, and nothing is placed on a timeline by guesswork.

Grounding is the path to reach for when a value repeats across your data; editing a cell is for a one-off.
